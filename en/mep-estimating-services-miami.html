MEP estimating services in Miami and South Florida

Mechanical, electrical and plumbing priced as one coordinated package — the trades that carry the most cost risk and cause the most conflict when they are priced in isolation.

Florida MEP estimation done by estimators who price the three trades against each other rather than in three separate silos. Mechanical, electrical and plumbing arrive as three coordinated estimates rather than one lump sum, each broken out by CSI division, so you can hand a subcontractor their scope without exposing anyone else's price. It is the broadest package inside our construction estimating service for Miami-Dade, and the one general contractors ask for most.

Every project inside Miami-Dade also sits in the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone, which drives equipment ratings, rooftop restraint and the inspection sequencing that decides your schedule — and with it your general conditions.

What a Miami MEP package covers, trade by trade

  • Mechanical / HVAC. Air handlers, condensing units, VRF systems, ductwork by gauge and size, diffusers, dampers, insulation, and the condensate and refrigerant piping that gets left out of quick takeoffs.
  • Electrical. Service entrance, switchgear, panelboards, feeders, branch circuiting by home run, lighting fixtures and controls, devices, fire alarm and low-voltage rough-in.
  • Plumbing. Underground and above-ground rough-in, domestic water, sanitary and storm, gas piping, fixtures and trim, water heaters and pumps.
  • Coordination allowances. Where ductwork, conduit and piping compete for the same ceiling space, we price the offsets and supports that the drawings imply but do not show.
  • Access, rigging and lead times. Switchgear and rooftop units are the long poles on a South Florida schedule, and landing them on a tight urban site is a cost, not a detail.
  • Commissioning scope. Test and balance, controls integration and the startup sequence, priced rather than left for the closeout argument.
  • Labor hours visible. Crew composition and productivity rates are shown per line rather than buried in a lump sum, so you can test them against your own numbers.

How three trades get measured without colliding

Each trade keeps its own units. Ductwork is quantified by size and gauge with fittings measured as fittings, not smoothed into a centerline length; equipment goes in by each with its capacity, curb and structural support attached; refrigerant, condensate and hydronic piping by linear foot per size; insulation and liner by area. Electrical is counted by home run, device, fixture and gear, with conduit and conductor separate. Plumbing is measured by pipe size and material with the underground broken out on its own. Then the three are read against each other, which is where the value is: a duct main and a sprinkler main and a feeder all drawn through the same twelve inches of plenum is a coordination cost that a single-trade estimator never sees.

You receive one workbook with three chapters — CSI Divisions 21 to 23 and 26 — plus a PDF and the marked-up drawings, so any line traces back to the sheet it came from. Assumptions and exclusions are written per trade: design-build scope the drawings defer, utility charges, owner-supplied equipment, controls beyond the stated boundary. You can see the structure in our sample estimates.

Where MEP estimates go wrong

  • Three estimates that never met. Priced separately, nobody owns the space between them: equipment connections, housekeeping pads, access panels and the offsets needed to make the ceiling work.
  • Duct measured as centerline. Fittings, transitions and takeoffs are where the sheet metal labor lives, and a straight-run measurement quietly discounts them.
  • Dampers missed at rated assemblies. Fire and smoke dampers follow the rated walls on the life-safety plan, not the mechanical plan, so they are easy to leave out and expensive to add.
  • Refrigerant and condensate forgotten. Line sets, condensate routing and the pumps that go with them are small on the drawing and real on the invoice.
  • Roof scope underpriced. Curbs, restraints, structural support and the crane or rigging to land the unit are a package of their own in the HVHZ.
  • Test, balance and controls left to the end. They are a scope boundary, and unpriced boundaries become change orders.

Those are coordination failures rather than measurement failures, which is why they survive a spreadsheet check — the pattern is set out in seven ways to ruin a construction project.

Florida MEP estimation: what the code changes

  • Rooftop equipment has to be anchored for HVHZ wind loads. Curbs, restraints and attachment details on a Miami rooftop unit are heavier than the same unit in Orlando, and the difference is real money.
  • The code stack is Florida's, not the national base. The Florida Building Code Mechanical and Plumbing volumes, the National Electrical Code as adopted by Florida, the state energy provisions, and the Florida Fire Prevention Code for anything touching life safety — plus the HVHZ requirements that apply to everything in Miami-Dade and Broward.
  • Corrosion resistance near the coast. Coastal exposure drives material selection on supports, hangers and outdoor enclosures. Standard galvanized specifications do not always survive the spec review.
  • Generator and life-safety power. Miami-Dade high-rise and assembly occupancies frequently carry emergency power and transfer requirements that a national template will not include.
  • Cooling load is a year-round design. South Florida humidity and run hours drive equipment selection, condensate management and the energy-code items that come with them.
  • Inspection sequencing. HVHZ work carries more inspection hold points, which shows up in general conditions and in crew mobilization, not just in materials.
