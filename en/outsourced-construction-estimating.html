Outsourced construction estimating: a team you hire per bid

Outsourced construction estimating means buying the takeoff and the pricing of a bid per project instead of employing an estimator. Pr3cise does that work primarily for contractors in the State of Florida, and for clients across Europe and around the world, in English and Spanish: fixed fee, agreed delivery date, no salary between tenders.

Pr3cise is a remote estimating office — Clear Ascend OÜ, registered in Estonia — with no premises in any client country and no site visits. A contractor sends a drawing set for one bid; Pr3cise confirms the fee and the delivery date in writing, a technical architect with decades of building construction experience measures and prices the work directly, and the estimate comes back in Excel and PDF with the takeoff markups, normally within 24 to 72 hours. Nothing is committed beyond that project, so the arrangement can be tested on a single bid.

Contractors rarely go looking for outsourced construction estimating out of curiosity. They look because bid volume has outgrown the person doing the pricing, the estimator has left, or a tender landed that nobody has time to measure.

So the real question is not whether outsourcing works, but whether it works better than the alternative you are weighing — usually hiring, sometimes pricing it yourself at the weekend. This page answers that honestly, including the cases where the answer is no.

Pr3cise is an estimating office, not a staffing agency and not a recruiter: there are no CVs, no onboarding and no notice period — how the office works — and you can read real deliverables in the Pr3cise sample estimates.

In-house estimator or estimating company: the actual trade-off

  • Fixed cost against cost per bid. A salaried estimator costs the same in a quarter with two tenders as in one with twelve. Pr3cise ran the arithmetic of both options in in-house estimator vs outsourced estimating. An outsourced estimate is priced per project and stops when you stop bidding.
  • Capacity that moves with the pipeline. One package this week and five next changes nothing in your office. An estimator at capacity means declining work or rushing it, and rushed pricing is where margin goes.
  • Time to productive. Recruiting a good estimator takes months, and the months after that are spent learning your systems. An external estimating office starts on the next drawing set.
  • Trade breadth. Most individual estimators are strong in two or three trades and passable in the rest. Decades of building construction experience across architectural, structural and MEP scopes covers the whole building — which matters most on the trades you bid least often.
  • Auditable line-by-line discipline. Rushed internal pricing leaves arithmetic unverified and scope gaps buried in lump sums. At Pr3cise every estimate is prepared directly by a technical architect with colour-coded drawing markups and explicit written assumptions and exclusions.
  • Overhead you stop carrying. Takeoff software seats, cost-data subscriptions, training, holiday and sick cover, and the desk itself.

Work out your own break-even rather than trusting anyone's marketing: take the fully loaded annual cost of the post — salary, employment taxes, software, recruitment, cover — and set it against the number of estimates you genuinely need each month. Below a certain bid volume a hire never pays for itself; well above it, a hire plus overflow support beats either alone. When outsourcing is the wrong answer

It is worth saying plainly, because nobody selling this service says it. Outsourcing is a poor fit when your estimator is really a hybrid role — pricing in the morning, buying out subcontractors in the afternoon. You are not buying estimating then, you are buying a person, and no outside office replaces that.

It also fits badly when you need someone in the room through negotiation and buyout, when your pricing lives entirely in one person's head as proprietary assemblies nobody has written down, or when the client will not permit drawings to leave their systems. Say so in the first email and Pr3cise will tell you straight.

How a remote estimating team runs alongside your office

No onboarding, no systems to adopt, no standing meetings. Four steps per project, with direct technical architect continuity across every bid.

01

Brief and NDA

A non-disclosure agreement is signed before drawings move, on your paper if you have a standard one. You tell us the project, the trades, the tender date and whether to apply your rates or ours.

02

Scope, fee and date in writing

We read the set before quoting, then confirm what is included, the fixed fee and the delivery date in writing. No hourly billing, no timers. If the deadline is not realistic, you hear it now.

03

Measure, price, question

Quantities come off the drawings with every item marked on the sheet, then rates are applied with material, labour and equipment kept separate. Anything the drawings do not answer goes onto one batched question list.

04

Delivery and revision

Excel and PDF with the marked-up takeoff, in your template if you have one. We walk your team through it on request, adjust markups to your numbers, and reprice affected sections when an addendum lands.

Working across time zones without losing a day

This is the objection contractors raise first and stop raising after the first project. Our working day starts seven hours ahead of US Eastern Time for most of the year (six hours during seasonal DST transitions) and an hour ahead of Central European Time. A drawing set sent at the end of a Florida afternoon is measured overnight and waiting when the office opens; a European set sent at close of business is in progress before you arrive.

What makes it work is the discipline around the clock, not the clock itself. Questions are batched into one list, so you answer ten things once instead of one thing ten times. Assumptions are written down so work continues while an answer is pending. And nothing depends on a live call: the estimate, the markups and the question list are documents, which is why turnaround runs 24 to 72 hours.

Confidentiality: what happens to your drawings and your numbers

Handing an outside office a view of your bid is a real risk, and it deserves a real answer rather than a reassuring sentence.

  • An NDA before anything moves, on your standard form if you have one. If you do not, we provide one.
  • Your documents are used for your job only: not shown to another client, not reused as a template, not published as sample work without written permission. The samples in our portfolio are our own material.
  • We are not a contractor. Pr3cise does not bid, build or subcontract work, so there is no scenario in which we are across the table from you on a tender.
  • One bidder per tender. Tell us the project name at the briefing and we check it against live work. If we are already engaged on that tender, we decline yours and say why.
  • Files are returned or deleted on request once the job closes, and your rate library, if you ask us to price with it, is never applied to another client's work.

What you get, and who signs what

The deliverable is a working document: an Excel workbook and a PDF, structured by CSI MasterFormat division or the classification your market uses, with material, labour and equipment split on every line, waste factors per material, productivity in hours, and general conditions, overhead and profit broken out where you can edit them. The marked-up takeoff comes with it, so any line traces back to the sheet it was measured from, with the drawing stage and basis of measurement stated clearly on the cover.

Responsibility is worth stating precisely, because it is where outsourcing arrangements usually go vague. We are responsible for the estimate as prepared — the measurement, the build-up, the assumptions and the exclusions, all written down and all auditable. You are responsible for the bid. The markup, the risk allowance, any rate you substitute and the decision to submit belong to you. We are not the engineer or architect of record, we do not stamp drawings and we do not sign your tender.

Exclusions are listed rather than buried: fees an authority has not confirmed, utility connection charges, owner-supplied equipment, escalation past the validity date, bonds at rates we do not know, and scope the drawings leave to a design-build subcontractor. Standards and languages we work in

A remote team is only useful if it delivers in the standard your tender is written to. We work in English and Spanish, and the deliverable follows your tender: CSI MasterFormat divisions; an NRM2 or SMM bill of quantities, with NRM1 at cost-plan stage; mediciones by capítulos and partidas (BC3); a priced bill of quantities to POMI or NRM2 for FIDIC-based contracts; and, across Europe, pricing against the Eurocodes as adopted by the national annex where you are building.

Florida is our primary focus, and South Florida carries most of that work: everything in Miami-Dade falls inside the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone, which changes assemblies, product approvals and therefore quantities throughout a building, so it has a dedicated page on construction estimating in Miami. That is one specialism, not the boundary of the service — a tender anywhere in Europe or the rest of the world gets the same method and the same direct technical architect preparation.
