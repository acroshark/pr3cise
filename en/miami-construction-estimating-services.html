What Miami-Dade actually changes in a construction estimate

  • High-Velocity Hurricane Zone compliance. The HVHZ covers Miami-Dade and Broward counties, and it governs impact-rated openings, roof-to-wall connection density, enhanced fastening schedules and the product approvals that go with them. HVHZ is not a line item you add at the end — it changes assemblies throughout the building.
  • Notice of Acceptance constraints. Miami-Dade product control issues a Notice of Acceptance for products used in the HVHZ, each with its own number and expiry date, and the permit submittal has to carry them. A statewide product approval does not automatically satisfy the zone. That removes cheaper unapproved substitutes from the pricing set: we price the approved assembly your specification names rather than guessing a replacement.
  • Wind-borne debris protection. Openings need impact-rated glazing or approved shutters, and the choice between them is one of the largest single swings in a South Florida envelope budget. We price it per opening type, off the window and door schedule.
  • Permit fees priced as real money. Building permit fees are calculated from the declared construction value under the fee schedule of whichever authority has jurisdiction, and each municipality runs its own. It is the most commonly forgotten line in the budgets we are asked to review.
  • Recertification and milestone inspection work. Miami-Dade's building recertification program and Florida's milestone inspection requirements generate a steady stream of repair scopes, priced straight from the engineer's report and itemized so an owner or association sees what each recommendation costs.
  • Local labor and material rates. Miami-Dade wage rates and South Florida material pricing, cross-checked against live supplier quotes, not a national average that understates the job by double digits.
  • Coastal and flood-zone conditions. Elevation requirements, corrosion-resistant fastening, salt-air material selection, and a water table shallow enough across much of the county to put dewatering into the groundworks price.

None of that is exotic knowledge in Miami — your own project manager knows it. The problem is that generic estimating templates do not, which is how a job gets bid at a national average and built at a Miami price. How a Miami estimate gets built, in four steps

The same four steps on every job, one trade or the whole building. What we need from you is listed with each one, because a missing schedule is the usual reason an estimate slips.

01

Drawings and scope in

Send the drawing set, specifications and any addenda, plus the bid date and the trades you want priced. PDF is fine; CAD or a BIM model is better. We confirm the fixed fee and the delivery date in writing before anything starts, and we say so plainly if your deadline is not realistic.

02

Measurement and takeoff

Quantities come off the drawings on screen, sheet by sheet, with every measured item colour-marked on the plan. Areas, lengths, counts and volumes are kept in their own units rather than converted to a single lump. Drawing conflicts and scope gaps are logged as we find them, not swallowed.

03

Pricing and assumptions

Material, labor and equipment are priced separately on each line with South Florida rates, waste factors applied per material, and productivity shown in hours. Anything the drawings do not answer becomes a written assumption or an exclusion, so your risk is visible before bid day rather than after award.

04

Delivery and review

You receive the estimate in Excel and PDF with the marked-up takeoff, organized by CSI MasterFormat division. We walk you through it if you want, adjust markups to your numbers, and reprice affected sections when an addendum lands or the scope moves.

What you receive, and what the estimate leaves out

The deliverable is a working document, not a summary sheet. It arrives as an Excel workbook plus a PDF, structured by CSI MasterFormat division, with material, labor and equipment split on every line, quantities in their proper units, unit rates, waste factors, productivity hours, and general conditions, overhead and profit broken out where you can edit them. The marked-up drawings come with it, so any line can be traced to the sheet and the shape it was measured from. A number nobody can check is a number nobody can defend in a bid review.

Estimate class is stated rather than implied. A conceptual budget built on an early scheme is not a bid-level estimate, and pretending otherwise is how owners end up treating a guess as a commitment. We use the AACE International classification — Class 5 or 4 for order-of-magnitude work, Class 3 for a control budget, Class 2 or 1 for hard bidding — and the cover says which one you are holding.

Exclusions are written down. Fees the authority has not confirmed, utility connection charges, owner-supplied equipment, escalation beyond the stated validity date, bonds and insurance at rates we do not know, unforeseen ground conditions, and any scope the drawings leave to a design-build subcontractor: each is listed explicitly instead of hidden in a lump. When an addendum lands, we reprice the affected sections and reissue rather than patching a total.
